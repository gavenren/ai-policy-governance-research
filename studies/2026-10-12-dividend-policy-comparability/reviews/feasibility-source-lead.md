# Source-lead feasibility screen

**State: Working notes.** This note records exploratory reading, not numerical results. The source reading led to the S018 protocol. No model was imported, run or changed by this source agent. The initial screen had no study number. It was later selected as S018, a short source-specific audit.

## Decision and question

Proceed with a narrow replication comment. Do not present this as a new theory of basic income or a test of national fiscal viability.

The question is: **Which payment rule does each Four Pillars fiscal probability describe, and how much does that rule change the annual budget result on the same economic paths?** The two policies are a fixed nominal floor and the source's original rule for revenue-triggered increases. This is a comparison of named source policies. It is not an arbitrary sensitivity grid.

The source claims that automation-related taxes and other revenue growth can finance an income floor. This makes its payment commitment important for economic policy under severe work displacement. The released simulations stop well short of a model in which all wages disappear. The audit must not relabel their outcomes as forecasts of that condition.

## What static reading established

The whitepaper's abstract and Section 6.2 give a negative Year 10 median gap and a surplus probability above one-half. Both source reducers define the median from the same year's gap observations and surplus as a strictly positive annual gap. Those two summaries cannot describe the same observation array. Section 6.4 instead reports a positive median beside 55.7%. These are observations about source text and definitions. At the screen stage, neither source result had been reproduced.

The ten-year source applies a two-stage spending rule. Each year starts at the base floor cost. In years 5 onward, a strict revenue test can increase it by 12%. In years 8 onward, a second strict test can set it to 120% of base. The second test includes the first increase in its denominator. The next year starts from base again. Thus this rule can reverse an increase. It is neither a permanent ratchet nor price indexation.

The extended source leaves the floor cost at base. It retains the common parameter distributions and the same first-ten-year displacement formula, but changes the payment rule. A common seed does not pair all simulations across the two scripts: each twenty-year run consumes additional draws before the next path begins. Direct comparison of the two reported probabilities cannot isolate the spending-rule effect.

The strongest favorable reading is that the extended calculation is an intended fixed-floor case. Section 2.1 says the floor is not indexed and future increases are discretionary. Section 6.4's statement that parameter distributions are identical can be literally correct. This does not make the payment policies identical. The audit should separate a valid scenario from unclear attribution.

The April 19 post-launch note already calls for reconciliation of the older roughly 34% probability and 55.7%. It suggests different parameter sets as a possible cause. The source lead read the whole note. No claim that the discrepancy was concealed or previously unnoticed is justified. The limited increment is an executable assignment of results to policies and a comparison that holds economic paths fixed.

## Closest work and distinctness

Hoynes and Rothstein (2019), NBER Working Paper 25538, Sections III–IV and the research discussion, already show why a basic-income policy must specify its guarantee, phase-out, eligible population, financing and treatment of existing benefits. Their US evidence uses the 2017 CPS and is not a forward simulation of AI fiscal revenue. The source under audit cites this paper. Our read covered the transfer framework, costs, fiscal interpretation and pilot limits. We did not reproduce their estimates or claim a full read of every page. Their work rules out a novelty claim based on the simple fact that benefits cost money or that replacement and supplementation differ.

The OECD's May 2017 *Basic income as a policy option: Technical Background Note*, Sections 1 and 4, changes either benefit amounts or tax rates to make fiscal comparisons consistent. It distinguishes hypothetical family cases from a population model. This is direct prior support for naming the policy being compared. The source lead read printed pages 3–6 and 11–12 from the reviewer's retained official PDF. This is not a full report read.

Wenger's *The AGI Economy* repository README already compares fixed nominal transfers with income-indexed transfers and states its limits as an uncalibrated model. The source lead read the README, especially transfer indexation and limitations. The code was not acquired or run. That prior makes a general claim about the importance of indexation unsuitable as a new contribution here.

Within this programme, S004 studies real support under scarce goods, S005 studies public-dividend finance, and S016 audits the meaning of reported AI-output quantities. S018 uses the reproducibility approach of a source audit but concerns a different object: the payment rule attached to a budget probability. It does not add a new general economic mechanism. A short comment is proportionate to that increment.

## Boundaries and stop rule

The selected audit must preserve both unchanged scripts first, then compare both payment rules within each retained cohort. It must report the joint payment and fiscal outcomes. A smaller deficit produced by a smaller transfer is not a welfare improvement. The original distributions are assumptions, not a calibrated probability that a government will achieve a surplus.

Keep revenue feedback, price changes, household behavior, legal implementation, debt costs and retirement commitments outside this comparison. The wider corpus has unresolved issues and different draft details. This screen has not validated them and does not use them to enlarge the paper. The source's timeline, retirement model and tax alternative were read only to check scope. No fresh public population estimates or future AI predictions were produced.

If the unchanged source runs do not reproduce the relevant outputs, report that limit before attribution. If the audit adds only the already acknowledged table mismatch, reduce the output to a correction note. Do not pad it into a long paper. An independent review remains necessary after actual execution.
