# A Canadian Plan for a Secure Living After Wages
## Costs, funding, and an order of action

**Policy application · 12 October 2026 · All amounts in Canadian dollars**  
**Basis:** [A Secure Living After Wages](../../docs/POLICY_PAPER.md), which combines 15 studies in this repository.  
**Status:** AI-assisted policy draft with separate AI checks. This is a conditional budget analysis, not an official costing, a forecast of AI progress, or human peer review. Evidence was checked to 12 October 2026.

## The decision for Canada

Canada should prepare a payment system that does not depend on employment. It should also increase access to housing, care, and energy. Tax reform, service projects, and payment tests must start together. Relief should depend on need. A person should not have to prove that AI caused the loss of income.

This paper separates two decisions. A **limited transition programme** costs about **$21 billion a year**, before interest and a new GST rebate. A proposed tax package supplies about **$22.7 billion a year after that rebate** under the central assumptions. These are planning estimates. They need official costing before adoption.

A much larger promise has a different cost. An **additional $24,000 a year for every adult** costs about **$819.8 billion a year** in cash alone. In the central case of widespread wage loss, services, child support, administration, and lost federal receipts raise the need for new annual revenue to about **$1.10 trillion**. This would keep the starting federal deficit unchanged. It would not balance the budget.

The first tax package cannot fund the second promise. Canada can prepare now, but it must show how it will collect enough revenue before it calls a permanent universal payment fully funded.

## 1. Starting conditions and assumptions

The Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO) projects the following federal account for 2026–27. It already has a deficit. Existing receipts are therefore not spare money for a new programme. Planned savings already included in this account cannot be counted again. These values come from one forecast, not a mixture of budget baselines. [PBO, June 2026 outlook](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2627-002-S--economic-fiscal-outlook-june-2026--perspectives-economiques-financieres-juin-2026)

| Starting value | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Federal revenue, 2026–27 | $528.3bn |
| Federal expense, after the actuarial adjustment | $600.1bn |
| Federal deficit | $71.8bn |
| Personal income tax, included in revenue | $248.3bn |
| Corporate income tax, included in revenue | $101.2bn |
| GST receipts, included in revenue | $52.8bn |
| Employment Insurance premiums, included in revenue | $33.9bn |
| Canada Child Benefit, included in expense | $30.9bn |
| Nominal GDP, calendar 2026 | $3,392bn |

Statistics Canada estimates **34,158,557 adults aged 18 or older** and **7,639,850 children** on 1 July 2026. These are preliminary population estimates. They are not counts of people who meet a benefit law's residence or other rules. The universal case below assumes that all these people qualify. [Statistics Canada, Table 17-10-0005-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1710000501)

The constructed budgets use constant 2026 dollars. The nominal 2026–27 fiscal baseline is held fixed as an approximate 2026-price benchmark. No separate price adjustment is made. Population and real tax bases also stay fixed. The results show the scale of choices; they are not future federal budget forecasts. Calendar-year GDP is used only for scale. It is not fiscal-year GDP.

The plan makes no claim that complete wage loss will occur, or that it will occur on this schedule. Being better at tasks does not by itself end all paid human activity. The severe case asks what follows **if wages cease to support most people**. Neither $12,000 nor $24,000 is a measured adequate living standard. Canada must test amounts against local housing costs, household size, disability, and care needs.

## 2. A limited programme that Canada could prepare first

The first programme would top up low incomes while payment access and essential supply improve. The cost case assumes one million adult recipients and an average annual payment of $12,000. These are selected planning values. They are not a forecast of applications or a proposed limit of one million eligible people. An entitlement must have rules based on need, with a budget that responds when more people qualify.

| New item | Full-year cost | What the allowance covers |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Income support | $12bn | One million adults × an average $12,000 |
| Extra needs and service operation | $3bn | Disability and care gaps; operation of added services |
| Administration and access | $1bn | Enrolment, appeals, payment backup, collection work, and payment losses |
| Essential capital grants | $5bn | Housing, care facilities, and energy or other local constraints |
| **Annual direct spending** | **$21bn** | Before interest and the separate new GST rebate |
| Initial setup, once | $2bn | Systems, legal changes, and tests |
| Initial cash reserve, once | $12bn | A financial asset held for payment interruptions |

All lines are **new**. For this costing, new income support is tax-free and does not reduce existing benefits. Those terms need legislation and agreements. Existing benefits continue, so there are no claimed benefit savings. The $5 billion is a federal grant expense, even where the recipient builds a long-lived asset. It does not become a free federal investment return. The reserve is cash held aside. It is not annual programme spending and cannot also pay the normal budget without being drawn down.

The service allowance is a starting budget, not an estimate of every unmet need. For example, CMHC's fall 2026 housing analysis estimates a need for about 417,000–469,000 annual starts through 2036 under its affordability scenario. This paper does not claim that a $5 billion grant line will deliver that result. Projects need local plans, land, construction capacity, and later operating funds. [CMHC, Housing Supply Report](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/professionals/housing-markets-data-and-research/market-reports/housing-market/housing-supply-report)

### Proposed funding

Two tax changes provide a transparent first estimate. Neither is an enacted measure or an official revenue estimate for this plan.

First, add a surcharge equal to **20% of existing federal corporate income-tax liability**. A firm that owes $100 would owe an extra $20 before any redesign. This is not a 20 percentage point increase in the corporate tax rate. The case includes firms at the small-business rate. An exemption would reduce the estimate. Canada has several corporate rates, so dividing all corporate receipts by the general rate would be misleading. [CRA, corporate tax rates](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/corporations/corporation-tax-rates.html)

Second, raise the federal GST from **5% to 6%**, with a new rebate for low-income people. The 5% starting rate is current law. The extra point and rebate are proposals. [Excise Tax Act, section 165](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/e-15/section-165.html)

| Annual funding calculation | Central case |
| --- | ---: |
| Corporate surcharge: $101.2bn × 20%, less a selected 20% receipt reduction | $16.192bn |
| Extra GST: $52.8bn × 1/5, less a selected 10% receipt reduction | $9.504bn |
| New, additional GST rebate | −$3.000bn |
| **Net new resources** | **$22.696bn** |
| Direct spending from the previous table | −$21.000bn |
| **Amount left before interest** | **$1.696bn** |

The GST calculation scales **existing net receipts**, which already reflect credits and rebates. It is not a measurement of taxable consumption. The extra $3 billion rebate is additional to existing support. It must reach low-income people outside the one million income-support recipients as well. Its adequacy has not been proved. A household analysis may require a larger rebate and thus less money for other uses.

The receipt reductions are selected stress assumptions. They are not estimated confidence bounds. With no extra reductions, net resources are $27.8 billion. With corporate receipts reduced by 40% and extra GST receipts by 20%, resources fall to $17.6 billion. The lower case is not a guaranteed minimum.

Finance Canada and PBO should cost the package together. The analysis must include changes in prices, investment, avoidance, personal taxes, and corporate tax integration. Separate tax estimates cannot safely be added without checking these effects. The model does not measure them. It also counts no extra money from tariffs, better compliance, future public-fund returns, or foreign transfers.

## 3. Timing, cash, and debt in the first five years

Planning before April 2027 is assumed to use existing departmental budgets. Any extra preparation appropriation must be added. The preparation year is 2027–28. It includes $5 billion in capital grants, $1 billion in administration, and $2 billion in setup. It assumes half a year of new receipts and the new rebate. The $12 billion reserve is established that year. Scheduled income payments and extra service operation begin in 2028–29.

This schedule is **not a reason to delay urgent relief**. Existing lawful support should respond at once. Any extra emergency appropriation before 2028–29 is outside this scheduled budget and must be added explicitly.

The model assumes a 2% real borrowing rate and zero real return on the cash reserve. It charges a full year's interest on opening programme debt and any positive new financing need before interest. Annual surpluses repay that programme debt. The reserve stays intact. This is a simplified resource and cash account, not a forecast under Canada's public-sector accounting standards.

| Fiscal year | Net new resources after rebate | Direct spending before interest | Interest | Cash added to reserve | New borrowing / (repayment) | Closing programme debt |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2027–28 | $11.35bn | $8.00bn | $0.17bn | $12.00bn | $8.83bn | $8.83bn |
| 2028–29 | $22.70bn | $21.00bn | $0.18bn | — | ($1.52bn) | $7.31bn |
| 2029–30 | $22.70bn | $21.00bn | $0.15bn | — | ($1.55bn) | $5.76bn |
| 2030–31 | $22.70bn | $21.00bn | $0.12bn | — | ($1.58bn) | $4.17bn |
| 2031–32 | $22.70bn | $21.00bn | $0.08bn | — | ($1.61bn) | $2.56bn |

Borrowing is a cash source, not tax revenue. The first year's reserve purchase explains why borrowing rises even though this programme improves the budget balance before the reserve addition. The reserve remains $12 billion at the end of every modelled year. It equals nine months of the $16 billion recurring income, needs, and administration budget. It does not prove that each payment can arrive on time. A monthly cash test is still needed.

These are changes caused by this programme only. They do not pay off Canada's existing debt or remove its starting deficit. The central full-year margin is small. It cannot support large uncosted increases.

| Stress case | Change in the federal deficit in 2028–29* | Programme debt at end of 2031–32 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Central assumptions | −$1.52bn | $2.56bn |
| Lower receipts | +$3.70bn | $26.70bn |
| Two million recipients | +$10.69bn | $52.87bn |
| 4% real borrowing rate | −$1.34bn | $3.32bn |
| Lower receipts, two million recipients, and 4% rate | +$16.49bn | $81.68bn |

*Positive means a larger deficit; negative means a smaller deficit. The recipient stress holds other cost allowances fixed, which may understate the cost of expansion. The model does not spend the reserve or show how to refill it after a shock.*

If demand grows, government must change funding or approve a larger budget. It must not present a fixed appropriation as a complete guarantee. Parliament should review receipts, applications, payment delays, and essential prices at least quarterly during the first two years.

## 4. If wages fail across the economy

The severe case tests a precisely defined promise: **an additional tax-free $24,000 each year for every counted adult**, with the payment excluded from other benefit income tests. Existing benefits stay in place. This is a large gross commitment, not the minimum cost of ensuring $24,000 in total income. Recovering payments from people with other income could lower net cost, but that requires a different policy and a separate estimate.

The case also assumes that all counted children receive current maximum Canada Child Benefit (CCB) rates: $8,157 below age six and $6,883 at ages six to seventeen. The actual CCB is income-tested and uses prior-year family income. Zero wages alone does not imply that every family receives the maximum; investment income and eligibility still matter. Universal maximum coverage here is an explicit policy assumption. [CRA, July 2026–June 2027 CCB rates](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/child-family-benefits/canada-child-benefit/how-much.html)

| Annual federal stress account | Central assumption or result |
| --- | ---: |
| Adult cash: 34,158,557 × $24,000 | $819.81bn |
| Extra child support: $55.48bn total less $30.9bn already budgeted | $24.58bn |
| New service allowance, selected assumption | $15.00bn |
| New administration, assumed at 1% of adult cash | $8.20bn |
| **New annual spending** | **$867.58bn** |
| Lost personal income tax: assumed 80% of $248.3bn | $198.64bn |
| Lost EI premiums: assumed 100% of $33.9bn | $33.90bn |
| **New annual revenue needed to keep the $71.8bn starting deficit** | **$1,100.12bn** |
| **New annual revenue needed to balance the federal budget** | **$1,171.92bn** |

The assumed 80% personal-tax loss is not a measured share due to wages. Personal tax also comes from pensions and investments. The model tests losses of 50%, 80%, and 100%. It holds all other baseline revenue fixed. It assumes no savings from existing benefits and no tax recovery from the new payment. It also leaves existing spending fixed, apart from the stated additions.

The calculation is simple: new spending plus lost receipts requires $1,100.12 billion in new revenue. Remaining baseline revenue is $295.76 billion. Total federal expense becomes $1,467.68 billion. Their difference is $1,171.92 billion before new revenue. The transition tax package is not automatically added; its present tax bases may not survive this change.

A lower adult payment of $18,000 costs $614.9 billion gross and requires $893.1 billion in new revenue under the same central tax-loss assumption. A $30,000 payment costs $1,024.8 billion gross and requires $1,307.1 billion. These are choices about the promise, not predictions about available money.

At the baseline GDP, the central $1.10 trillion requirement equals about 32.4% of GDP **in additional revenue**. Doubling the GDP denominator reduces that ratio to 16.2%. This arithmetic does not predict growth or show that government can tax it. Production, taxable income, collected receipts, and cash available to pay benefits are different quantities.

A two-month reserve for the adult payment alone would hold $136.6 billion. This is a stock of cash, not another annual benefit expense. It needs its own funding and timing test. It cannot solve a permanent revenue gap.

### What this federal account leaves out

The account is not the total cost for all Canadian governments. Provincial and local revenue can also fall. For scale, Statistics Canada records $158.351 billion in taxes on individuals for consolidated provincial, territorial, and local governments in reference year 2024. That historical amount includes investment income and is not a 2026–27 wage-tax forecast. It must not simply be added to the federal loss. [Statistics Canada, Table 10-10-0147-01](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=1010014701)

The model does not estimate losses of CPP or QPP contributions, higher claims on other benefits, future construction costs, or interest on new borrowing in the severe case. These omissions can materially change the result. Keeping an existing spending line fixed does not prove that it will meet all future claims.

PBO's 2025 basic-income study is a useful comparison, but it costs a different promise in a wage economy. Its family income tests, payment withdrawal, and removal of benefits and tax credits change the cost. Its small net fiscal cost cannot be carried into this case, where wage-tax offsets are weak and existing benefits remain. [PBO, basic-income analysis, February 2025](https://www.pbo-dpb.ca/en/publications/RP-2425-029-S--distributional-analysis-national-guaranteed-basic-income-update--analyse-distributive-un-revenu-base-garanti-echelle-nationale-mise-jour)

## 5. How to fund a permanent system

The federal and provincial governments need a joint plan to replace revenue tied to wages. It should test taxes on corporate profits and investment income, a broader consumption base with rebates, and taxes on large wealth and land values. The mix must reflect who owns AI income, where it is taxed, and who bears each tax. International agreements can help collect tax on cross-border profits. They cannot be booked as money before duties and collection routes exist.

These are candidates for a new costing, not an extra list of receipts already available. Corporate profits may rise, but competition, foreign ownership, and avoidance may limit Canadian collection. Consumption taxes can reach some returns missed by profit taxes, but they also raise living costs. Rebates and payment amounts must therefore be assessed together.

Public ownership can provide a later income stream. Acquiring assets has a cost and carries risk. The budget must show the purchase, debt service, fees, losses, and cash that can safely be paid out. This paper counts no investment return before those steps. CPP and QPP assets already support their contributors and beneficiaries. They are not a free source for a new federal dividend. [Canada Pension Plan Investment Board Act, section 5](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-8.3/section-5.html); [Retraite Québec, QPP](https://www.retraitequebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/programs/quebec-pension-plan)

Temporary borrowing can protect people during a shock while new taxes take effect. It cannot by itself fund an unexplained permanent gap. A larger permanent promise needs a published account that identifies recurring net revenue, service costs, debt costs, and a funded cash reserve. Discretionary increases should pass that test. Existing legal payment rights and emergency relief need their own funded rules.

## 6. Who acts, and when

The following dates are proposed targets from October 2026. They do not describe laws already passed. Parliament must authorize federal taxes and spending. Provinces and territories must agree on benefit interactions and services within their responsibilities. First Nations, Inuit, and Métis governments need direct participation; provincial consent cannot replace it.

| Period | Lead bodies and actions | Required decision or result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **October 2026–March 2027** | Finance Canada leads a joint costing. CRA and Service Canada map payment gaps. Provinces, territories, local authorities, and Indigenous governments identify service constraints. | Define eligibility, the payment unit, income tests, appeal rights, and emergency funding. Publish household effects and a full tax estimate before passing the permanent package. |
| **2027–28: prepare and test** | Parliament considers revenue and spending laws. CRA tests collection and enrolment. Service agencies test offline access, urgent payments, and backups. Partner governments start costed essential projects. | Use the preparation-year budget only after approval. Negotiate protection against provincial benefit loss. Set up the reserve and publish monthly cash tests. |
| **2028–29: first full year** | Payment agencies start the limited income top-up. Partner governments operate added services. Finance reports receipts and costs. | Plan for one million recipients at an average $12,000. Treat these as budget assumptions, not an eligibility cap. Report access failures and actual costs each quarter. |
| **2029–30 to 2031–32: review and adjust** | Parliament reviews payment levels and funding each year. Provincial and territorial legislatures review related services and benefits. Independent auditors check delivery. | Expand only with a new cost and supply plan. Rebuild any used reserve. Develop the joint response to lasting wage-tax loss. |
| **At any time: severe income loss** | Governments use existing support and seek extra authority and funds where needed. | Provide relief based on current need. Publish an emergency budget that includes lost receipts. Do not wait for a final finding that AI caused the shock. |

Canada can build on existing institutions, but must not assume that they reach everyone. CRA plans pre-filled returns for invited people from March 2027. Users must still review and submit them. That is not universal automatic enrolment. Payment access also needs in-person, telephone, and other non-digital routes. [CRA, easier tax filing](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/campaigns/easier-tax-filing.html)

Provinces and territories deliver most health services. Publicly insured hospital and physician care already has an employment-independent basis. The plan should protect this system and address remaining gaps in care and coverage. It should not budget as if Canada must first replace employer-based hospital insurance. [Health Canada, the health system](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/canada-health-care-system.html)

A new cash benefit must not remove disability, drug, housing, or social-assistance support by accident. Governments should agree on treatment of the new payment before launch and provide remedies when an error occurs. The Canada Social Transfer is largely unconditional; the proposed protections are not already binding conditions of that transfer. They require agreements or legal changes. [Finance Canada, Canada Social Transfer](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/programs/federal-transfers/canada-social-transfer.html)

## 7. Conditions for a sound decision

Before adoption, Canada needs a household-level model of taxes and benefits, provincial accounts, service plans, and monthly payment tests. It should publish who gains, who loses, and how results change with rent, disability, family structure, and investment income. Averages alone cannot establish an adequate standard of living.

The useful result of this analysis is a separation of choices. Canada has a limited preparation route with visible costs and a proposed revenue source. A universal additional payment after widespread wage loss is a much larger fiscal decision. It needs a new division of economic returns and a new agreement across governments. Higher AI output alone does not supply either one.
