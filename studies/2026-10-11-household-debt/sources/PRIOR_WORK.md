# Closest work and the bounded contribution

The search record is in [searches.md](../logs/searches.md). This was a targeted search using original studies and official source pages, with reference following. It was not a systematic review. The source IDs below resolve to the [manifest](manifest.json) and retained extracts. Full text was unavailable for P3 and P5; that gap limits the originality assessment.

| Source and scope read | What is already established | What this study can still add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1, Helppie-McFall and Hsu (2020), PDF pp. 5–16 | SCF income-loss vulnerability, savings, and housing/debt obligations are studied together. | The current question is the difference between two programme success definitions, not the existence of payment vulnerability. |
| P2, Ganong and Noel (2020), PDF pp. 2–7 | Payment timing and the stock of debt have different effects in mortgage-policy evidence. | The current paper separates payment amounts from balances but supplies no new causal evidence. |
| P3, Laing (2023), abstract only | UBI design and debtor protection are already connected in legal-policy research. | A new debtor-protection principle is not claimed. The numerical diagnostic is conditional on a specific resource definition. |
| P4, Bhutta et al. (2019), PDF pp. 2–6 | Household debt has already been stress-tested under income and asset-price shocks. | This paper does not estimate delinquency; it measures paired classifications under an accounting experiment. |
| P5, Bhutta et al. (2020), abstract only | SCF savings, income and expenses have already been used to simulate cash support during unemployment. | The general simulation method is not new. A full-text comparison could narrow the remaining claim further. |
| P6, Aylward et al. (2021), full article | Nonemployment income and liquid assets can be used to compare the duration of consumption support, with and without transfers. | Asset-buffer calculations here are a sensitivity check, not a distinct invention. |

The proposed addition is a public diagnostic that distinguishes a gross-resource guarantee from a guarantee after the inherited payment burden, under an explicit cessation of recorded wage income. It reports their paired coverage difference and the distribution of extra support needed by an idealized targeted programme. It also shows why removing that gap is not a neutral definition of need: payment burdens reflect past borrowing, and tenants can face omitted costs.

The public survey vintage makes the illustration current; it does not by itself establish novelty. The study does not prove that this diagnostic has never appeared before. It offers a transparent implementation, explicit policy distinction, and reproducible distributional evidence. The work differs from the repository's fiscal, reserve, import, benefit-queue, asset-acquisition, international-agreement, and ownership-authority studies. Those earlier papers do not use public family microdata to compare these two guarantees.

No source is treated as an endorsement. Federal Reserve staff research is preliminary or authorial work, not a policy statement by the Board. This study makes no claim of outside author clearance, journal submission, or human peer review.
