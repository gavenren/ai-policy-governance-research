### Abstract

An income guarantee can meet its stated payment target while leaving families unable to meet the same cash threshold after debt service. This paper measures that difference in a fixed-contract stress test using the 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances. The main experiment removes recorded wage income, retains other recorded income, and adds a monthly transfer of $2,000 per family. It then compares resources with an illustrative $1,000 threshold before and after the SCF debt-service estimate. Every family passes the first test by construction. An estimated 22.9 percent fail the second, with a survey standard error of 0.6 percentage points. The average remaining shortfall is $402 per month across all families. The result rises when more income sources are removed or rent is included, and falls when debt payments are reduced. A separate comparison of idealized targeted programmes shows how a guarantee defined after debt service can become a large commitment to support existing contracts. These are conditional calculations, not recommended benefit levels or estimates of future fiscal cost. A later equal-budget extension shows that adult-based payments can increase the family failure rate while reducing the person-weighted failure rate and mean dollar shortfall. Their implication is that post-labor policy must specify whether it protects an income amount, consumption, or contractual payments, and how it counts families of different sizes. Those promises require different institutions and distribute support differently.

### 1. An income payment does not settle the debt question

Suppose that a family loses its wages but retains $500 a month from other sources. A new programme pays the family $2,000 a month. If success means having at least $1,000 of monthly resources, the programme appears to have succeeded. Yet the family also has $1,800 in debt payments. After those payments, only $700 remains. Another family with the same income and no debt has $2,500 left. Equal transfers have not produced equal amounts available for other uses.

The example does not show that the government should pay the first family's debts. Its borrowing may have financed a useful home, an expensive discretionary purchase, or a business asset. The second family may pay rent, face high medical costs, or have more dependants. A fair policy cannot be inferred from the debt balance alone. The example instead identifies a choice that an income proposal must make: are existing contracts to be serviced in full, reduced, or allowed to default when the income that supported them disappears?

That choice matters in a scenario where human labor no longer earns competitive wages. Loans made before the transition can depend on a stream of earnings that no longer exists. New AI production may make society richer while leaving those promises unchanged. A public dividend distributes some of the new output. It does not, by itself, determine how old claims rank against current consumption needs.

This paper studies the immediate accounting problem. It removes wage income from a recent household balance-sheet survey while holding debt payments fixed. The exercise asks how a programme's measured coverage changes when the analyst moves from resources received to resources remaining after debt service. It also calculates the extra transfer required by an idealized guarantee that expressly protects the latter amount.

The distinction is relevant even if permanent mass displacement never occurs. A policy that reports only receipt or gross income can miss important differences in the uses to which that income is committed. However, the analysis is not an estimate of present hardship. Families can sell assets, change spending, refinance, negotiate, or default. Taxes and other obligations also matter. The stress test holds these responses outside its boundary so the consequences of the chosen payment rule can be examined directly.

The main finding is quantitative but conditional. Under the specified wage-loss experiment, a $2,000 monthly family transfer puts all families above a $1,000 gross-resource threshold by construction. Recorded debt payments bring 22.9 percent below it. Among families with positive recorded wages, the share is 32.5 percent. The difference is large enough to change the description of what the programme has accomplished. It is not evidence that this share of families would default after an AI transition.

The analysis also gives reasons not to turn its after-debt measure into the sole policy objective. Adding recorded rent raises the affected share to 36.1 percent. A debt-only standard can overlook tenants and reward families that entered the transition with larger payment obligations. A government therefore needs an explicit decision about consumption protection and debt resolution, rather than an automatic commitment to reimburse every existing contract.

### 2. The contribution in relation to earlier research

Research on household resilience already shows that an income shock must be assessed against both resources and obligations. Helppie-McFall and Hsu (2020) use the Survey of Consumer Finances to compare the financial position of workers who faced different risks during pandemic restrictions. They examine liquid assets and rent plus debt payments relative to income. Their work establishes that the ability to withstand lost earnings varies with financial position as well as occupational exposure. This paper does not introduce that idea. [P1]

Bhutta and colleagues (2020) use SCF data to assess how CARES Act cash support could help families cover recurring expenses during unemployment. Their abstract makes the close relationship to the present exercise clear. Full-text access was not available in this research session, so this paper does not claim a detailed methodological difference from every part of their study. A related original calculation by Aylward and colleagues (2021), using SIPP data, measures how nonemployment income and liquid assets can sustain consumption after employment income ends. Temporary resilience is established prior work. [P5, P6]

Debt balances and current payments must also be distinguished. Ganong and Noel (2020) use mortgage modifications to separate changes in long-term wealth from changes in near-term liquidity. Their results concern specific borrowers and policy designs, rather than a permanent end to labor income. They nevertheless provide a strong reason to study scheduled payments directly. A reduction in principal need not give a family more cash this month if its payment is unchanged. Conversely, a payment extension can improve current cash flow without removing the obligation. [P2]

The interaction between basic income and debt protection is also not new. Laing (2023) argues that a UBI proposal must consider creditor seizure of payments. Only the article's abstract was accessible here. It is sufficient to reject a broad novelty claim about bringing debt into the basic-income debate; it is not sufficient to evaluate the article's detailed legal argument. This study does not examine garnishment law or assert that any present benefit is legally protected. [P3]

Finally, household debt stress tests can estimate default under macroeconomic shocks. Bhutta and colleagues (2019) use credit records and county-level conditions to model delinquency. The present calculation has a different purpose and cannot provide that predictive result. It measures a programme-accounting discrepancy under fixed payments. Passing or failing its threshold is not a behavioral prediction. [P4]

The limited contribution is to make that discrepancy the object of the analysis and to connect it to the wording of a post-labor income promise. The same household records are evaluated under a gross-resource test, an after-payment test, and two idealized top-up rules. A new survey year alone would not justify a separate paper. The useful addition is the comparison of what the programme would claim to protect, who falls between those claims, and which existing obligations a stronger guarantee would support.

This contribution is also distinct from the earlier studies in this repository. S004 examines whether production and scarce goods can support a consumption floor. S005 examines the government's cash reserve, S007 examines payment delivery, and S008 examines the acquisition of public capital. Here the transfer has already arrived. The question is what remains after a family meets earlier financial commitments. The literature search was bounded and cannot establish that no earlier work has made the same comparison.

### 3. Data, timing, and the limits of the experiment

The data come from the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances public summary extract, released on 9 October 2026. The public file contains 4,360 families. Each appears in five completed versions of the data, called implicates, to represent uncertainty about imputed values. The resulting 21,800 records are not 21,800 independent families. The analysis uses all five versions and the survey's 999 replicate weights. [D1–D6]

The unit is the SCF family, or primary economic unit. It is not necessarily every person living at one address. The paper uses a uniform amount per family because the selected summary data do not support a complete adult-level benefit calculation. It does not infer family size from marital status and the number of children. A $2,000 family transfer should therefore not be read as a proposal for a $2,000 payment to each adult. Section 8 adds a separately planned comparison using the full public roster; it does not alter the original calculations.

Three definitions require particular care. First, income is before tax. The remaining amount after debt payments is still before tax and before most ordinary expenses. It is not disposable income or a measure of consumption. Second, the income questions refer principally to the previous calendar year, while debt payments describe obligations near the interview. The official extract places monetary variables in 2025 dollars, but inflation adjustment does not remove this timing difference. Third, reported total income and its components need not sum exactly. The official macro documents both this issue and the inclusion of pension-account withdrawals. [D4]

The main stress therefore uses a deliberately simple rule. It subtracts recorded wage income from recorded total income, sets any negative remainder to zero, and divides by twelve. Other recorded flows remain available. These include public transfers, retirement income, business and property income, capital gains, and pension withdrawals. Their continued availability is an assumption, not a forecast. In particular, a withdrawal from an asset is not an indefinitely renewable income stream.

Setting a negative remainder to zero affects an estimated 2.15 percent of families in the wage-only experiment. This can reflect losses in other income components as well as differences between components and the reported total. The rule gives such families zero remaining resources rather than treating a negative annual total as a continuing monthly cash claim. It is optimistic relative to retaining those losses. The incidence is reported so the adjustment is visible.

A wider-flow experiment also removes positive business/farm/self-employment income, positive capital gains, and pension-account withdrawals. The business measure includes some rental income; it is not a pure labor-income measure. This case is consequently labeled a wider loss of recorded flows. It cannot be interpreted as a cleaner estimate of full automation. Its zero bound affects 6.52 percent of families. A no-wage-loss benchmark retains total recorded income.

The payment measure is TPAY, the official estimate of monthly debt service. It combines reported and constructed amounts. The summary macro assumes monthly credit-card payments of 2.5 percent of balances, buy-now-pay-later payments of 33 percent, and margin-loan interest at an 8 percent annual rate; payments on a residual other-loan category are set to zero. Some reported mortgage payments can include taxes or insurance held in escrow. TPAY is therefore a payment-burden proxy, not an observed list of contractual minimums. A proportional cut in TPAY is a mechanical sensitivity and need not correspond to a feasible reduction in creditor claims. [D3, D4]

Rent is separate and enters a stated sensitivity check. Assets and the payment estimate remain fixed at their survey values. The model does not create new credit, sell housing, assume refinancing, or adjust debts for inflation after the transition. The calculation uses the observed balance sheet and the official payment construction to approximate the inherited position. Its contract-policy interpretation remains conditional on that measurement limit.

The survey's nonrandom sampling makes weights essential. Point estimates are averages of estimates from the five implicates. The summary weight is already divided by five; the analysis restores that factor when calculating a total within one implicate. Sampling uncertainty follows the official codebook: it combines variation across the 999 weighted replicates of the first implicate with imputation variation across the five implicates. Missing replicate-weight cells represent families not selected in that replicate and are set to zero, as the codebook instructs. Appendix A gives the calculation. [D3, D5]

Two checks help establish that the data have been read correctly. Mean before-tax family income reproduces the public historical table's $144,630 after rounding. The share with debt reproduces 76.9879 percent after rounding. These checks support the weighting and units; they do not validate the hypothetical wage-loss scenario. The public extract omits seven interviewed families for disclosure protection, and the results describe the public file rather than unreleased records. [D6, D8]

### 4. What does the programme promise to protect?

Let $N_i$ be family $i$'s remaining monthly gross resources under a selected income experiment. Let $D_i$ be its estimated monthly debt payment, $U$ a uniform transfer, and $F$ an illustrative resource threshold. The family passes the gross-resource test when $N_i+U\geq F$. It passes the after-payment test when $N_i+U-D_i\geq F$.

Since debt payments are nonnegative, the second group is a subset of the first. The difference in coverage is the share of families for which the two descriptions disagree. The amount needed to bring a family up to the after-payment threshold is

$$
S_i(U,F)=\max\{F+D_i-N_i-U,0\}. \tag{1}
$$

This shortfall is an accounting quantity. It says how much additional cash would be needed if the family paid the full SCF debt-service estimate and if the specified threshold were the objective. It does not establish that the payments are affordable, that they will be made, or that society should cover them.

The main experiment sets $U=2{,}000$ and $F=1{,}000$ dollars per family per month. Because $N_i$ is bounded below by zero and $U$ exceeds $F$, the gross-resource pass rate is necessarily 100 percent. That result is a feature of the design, not empirical evidence that the transfer is adequate. The empirical question is how many families have estimated payment burdens large enough to make the after-payment result differ.

The thresholds are chosen for an understandable stress test, not derived from a poverty line or a cost-of-living budget. The analysis also uses transfers from zero to $4,000, residual thresholds of $500 and $1,500, and debt-payment reductions of one half or all of the recorded amount. The full grid includes cases that weaken the central result. No particular transfer is selected because it minimizes the reported discrepancy.

A second experiment compares two targeted guarantees. A gross-resource guarantee pays

$$
T_i^0=\max\{F-N_i,0\}.
$$

A guarantee that preserves the same amount after debt service pays

$$
T_i^1=\max\{F+D_i-N_i,0\}. \tag{2}
$$

These programmes assume perfect information and full take-up. They differ from the uniform $2,000 transfer. Their extra gross payment is $T_i^1-T_i^0$, which lies between zero and the family's debt service. Families whose remaining resources already cover both the threshold and debt receive no extra payment. For a family brought exactly to the gross threshold by the first programme, the extra amount equals its full debt service.

This identity exposes the policy choice. A promise to preserve a threshold after every estimated debt payment can commit the public budget to supporting those payments. It can also give more to a heavily indebted family than to a family with the same income and little debt. Whether that is desirable depends on the purposes of the debts, the distribution of other needs, creditor losses, and the available resolution procedures. None of these are resolved by the accounting identity.

### 5. The difference between receipt and remaining resources

Table 1 reports the wage-only experiment at the $1,000 threshold. Shares refer to all SCF families. Dollar shortfalls are monthly averages across all families, including those with no shortfall.

| Monthly family transfer | Pass gross-resource test | Pass after-payment test | Difference | Mean shortfall |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $0 | 56.0% | 45.4% | 10.6 percentage points | $1,192 |
| $1,000 | 100.0% | 61.2% | 38.8 percentage points | $701 |
| $2,000 | 100.0% | 77.1% | 22.9 percentage points | $402 |
| $3,000 | 100.0% | 86.7% | 13.3 percentage points | $221 |
| $4,000 | 100.0% | 93.7% | 6.3 percentage points | $125 |

The difference in classification is not monotone in the transfer. It rises between zero and $1,000 because more families cross the gross threshold without crossing the after-payment threshold. This is not a deterioration in family resources. Every larger transfer weakly reduces each family's shortfall. Once all families pass the gross test, further transfers reduce the classification gap. A policy dashboard that reports only that gap could therefore give a misleading impression of a programme expansion.

In the central case, the gap is 22.90 percent with a standard error of 0.58 percentage points. The $401.67 average shortfall has a standard error of $16.72. Approximately 13.3 percent have negative resources after the SCF debt-service estimate, even before applying the positive $1,000 threshold. The estimates concern the stipulated budget calculation. They should not be relabeled as poverty rates, missed-payment rates, or estimates of people unable to buy necessities.

The wage-loss assumption accounts for much of the difference. With no wage loss, only 0.4 percent fail the after-payment test under the same transfer and threshold, and the mean shortfall is $13. Under the wider-flow loss, the share is 29.9 percent and the mean shortfall is $598. This comparison shows sensitivity to the treatment of nonwage flows. It does not provide a probability range for future AI outcomes.

The discrepancy is also unevenly distributed. Table 2 uses the central case. Each row describes a separate subgroup; the rows are not intended to sum to the total.

| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Positive recorded wage income | 32.5% | $565 |
| No positive recorded wage income | 0.4% | $21 |
| Positive estimated debt payment | 30.5% | $534 |

All displayed groups contain more than 100 public families in the first implicate; the smallest has 1,179. Counts and weighted denominators for all five implicates are retained. Age and ownership differences are descriptive. They do not estimate an effect of becoming older or buying a home. Older families are less exposed to the specific removal of wages, while housing ownership often comes with payment obligations. The design preserves those existing associations.

The owner/nonowner comparison also demonstrates the risk of the chosen measure. A tenant's rent is not debt service. Excluding rent can make a tenant look protected when a substantial recurring housing payment remains. Table 2 should therefore not be used to conclude that owners deserve more support or that nonowners face little hardship.

### 6. Payment reductions, rent, and finite savings

Reducing payments changes the calculation directly. In the central wage-only case, cutting every estimated debt payment in half reduces the affected share from 22.9 to 11.1 percent. The mean shortfall falls from $402 to $92. Setting recorded payments to zero eliminates the debt-only shortfall because the uniform transfer already exceeds the threshold.

The last result is a useful boundary check, but it is not a free policy solution. Creditors lose or defer receipts. A lender may be a bank, a pension fund, another household, or the state. Forgiveness, subsidy, and maturity extension place costs in different places and at different times. This household calculation contains no lender balance sheet and cannot compare those options' total costs.

For a family's immediate budget, a dollar of payment relief and a dollar of additional cash have the same arithmetic effect, provided each is available at the relevant date. That equivalence does not extend to incentives, legal rights, future payments, or fiscal incidence. A temporary extension can move the obligation into a later period. If wage income never returns and no other income grows, that later claim still needs a resolution. A transition framed only as short-term unemployment can consequently choose an instrument with the wrong time horizon.

The threshold also matters. With the same $2,000 transfer and full debt payments, 17.9 percent fall below a $500 residual threshold, 22.9 percent fall below $1,000, and 29.8 percent fall below $1,500. Mean shortfalls are $299, $402, and $532. There is no single threshold-independent count of families that the transfer protects.

Including recorded rent alongside debt service raises the central shortfall share to 36.1 percent, with a standard error of 0.67 percentage points. The mean shortfall becomes $560, with a standard error of $18.36. This still excludes many expenses, taxes, differences in family size, and local prices. It remains an obligations test rather than a complete consumption standard. Its purpose is to expose how a debt-only definition can omit an important claim on income.

Liquid assets can cover some shortfalls for a time. In the central case, 16.7 percent of all families both have a shortfall and hold enough recorded liquid assets to cover three months of that fixed gap. For twelve months, the share is 10.0 percent. These are unconditional shares of all families, not percentages of affected families. The calculation uses assets once and assumes no additional needs or loss of asset access during the interval.

A family can therefore pass a short transition test while lacking a durable income arrangement. Savings can bridge a delay in a payment or a temporary interruption to work. They cannot finance an unchanged positive shortfall forever. The paper does not add liquid assets to annual income or treat the same stock as a fresh resource each year.

Finally, the dollar results could be driven by unusually large payments. Capping TPAY at its weighted 99th percentile within each implicate reduces the mean central shortfall from $402 to $374. The affected share changes only from 22.90 to 22.84 percent. The cap is roughly $9,200–$9,500 a month across implicates. This sensitivity indicates that very large payment amounts matter more for the mean gap than for the incidence result. The uncapped estimates remain the primary results.

### 7. The cost of defining a guarantee after debt service

The separate targeted-programme experiment asks what gross payment follows from two different definitions of the same $1,000 threshold. It does not start with the uniform $2,000 transfer. Table 3 reports annualized gross flows in 2025 dollars. Annualization means twelve times the fixed monthly calculation; it is not a forecast of the following year's budget.

| Recorded-income experiment | Gross-resource top-up | After-debt top-up | Extra gross payment |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| No wage loss | $12.1 billion | $70.4 billion | $58.3 billion |
| Wage income removed | $594.9 billion | $1,931.0 billion | $1,336.1 billion |
| Wider flow loss | $777.8 billion | $2,553.0 billion | $1,775.2 billion |

In the wage-only case, the extra annualized payment is $1.336 trillion, with a survey standard error of $34.1 billion. This is the difference between the two targeted top-ups. It is not the cost of closing the $402 average gap under the uniform-transfer experiment. Those results answer different questions and must not be combined as though they described one policy.

The gross-resource top-up reaches 44.0 percent of families in the wage-only experiment. The after-debt top-up reaches 54.6 percent. The extra programme therefore both raises payments to existing recipients and brings in families whose remaining gross resources exceed the threshold but do not cover it together with their recorded debts.

These totals are deliberately gross. Existing transfer income remains in N and already reduces the targeted top-up. The calculation includes no cuts to existing programmes, tax recovery from recipients, model of the tax base that finances the transfer, or administrative costs. It assumes that the remaining recorded income is available without changes induced by the policy. It also does not assume that every dollar paid to a creditor leaves the economy; creditor income can support other households and institutions. A macroeconomic or welfare comparison needs both sides of those transactions.

The result should instead be read as the scale of a conditional contractual promise in the current public data. If the state promises a fixed amount after the measured payment burden, the payment measure helps determine its liability. That link raises questions about verification and limits. Which debts qualify? Are new debts included after the programme starts? Can lenders increase payments because public support will rise? Are rent and other essential costs treated comparably? The accounting exercise cannot answer these questions, but it shows why the programme cannot omit them.

### 8. Extension: equal budgets can produce different answers

The original experiment gives the same amount to every family. That is a useful starting point, but a family can contain one person or several. A later extension, planned on 12 October before its outcomes were calculated, examines this choice. It keeps the original financial records and asks how the result changes when the payment depends on adult count or family size. The original results above remain unchanged.

Consider two families with no remaining income and $500 in monthly debt payments. One has one adult. The other has one adult and three children. Suppose that the combined transfer budget is $4,000. An equal family payment gives each $2,000. A size-adjusted threshold of $1,000 for the first family and $2,000 for the second leaves one family below its threshold after payment. If the budget is instead divided in proportion to the square root of family size, the payments are about $1,333 and $2,667. The first family now falls short and the second passes. One of two families still fails, but the number of people in failing families falls from four to one. The example does not choose a fair allocation. It shows why the payment rule, the needs assumption, and the unit counted in the outcome must be stated separately.

These choices are established parts of distributional analysis. The OECD's statistical framework discusses square-root scaling and warns against treating one scale as universally correct. It also notes that shared costs can extend beyond a family economic unit. Browne and Immervoll's comparative microsimulation already sets benefit amounts under a common fiscal constraint and distinguishes individual payments from family-based support. This extension applies those familiar distinctions to S011's debt-payment diagnostic. It does not propose a new equivalence scale. [E3, E4]

The full public SCF file supplies the fields omitted from the summary extract. Its financial-dependence flags identify PEU members, and its public ages preserve the boundary at 18. They do not preserve the age-14 boundary used in the modified OECD scale. Adult counts are therefore constructed directly from the roster, without treating marital status or the summary count of children as a substitute. Each of the 21,800 records joins to the original data. Two families in each implicate have size recorded as 12 or more. The primary comparison excludes them, leaving 4,358 families. Their weighted share ranges from 0.033 to 0.051 percent. An additional check includes them using the published, capped roster. [E1, E2, E5]

Three programmes share the same aggregate gross payment budget: $2,000 times the weighted number of included families per month. The first pays equally per family. The second pays equally per adult aged 18 or above and sums those payments within the family. The third pays in proportion to the square root of PEU size. Their estimated payment coefficients are $2,000 per family, about $1,173 per adult, and about $1,379 per square-root unit. The annual added payment total is about $3.238 trillion in the primary population under every rule. No taxes or reductions in existing benefits finance this amount in the experiment. Equal gross payments do not establish fiscal feasibility.

All three rules face the same threshold: $1,000 times the square root of PEU size. Thus a four-person family has a $2,000 threshold. This is an explicit policy assumption about size, not a measured cost-of-living budget. The PEU can exclude independent people at the same address, and the calculation does not measure shared housing costs across those units. It also does not measure each adult's control of the payment. Resources and payments are pooled within the PEU.

| Payment rule, equal aggregate budget | Families passing the gross test (%) | Families below the after-payment threshold (%) | People in families below that threshold (%) | Mean monthly shortfall per family ($) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Equal per family | 97.43 | 29.51 | 39.34 | 576.69 |
| Equal per adult | 96.55 | 31.61 | 37.11 | 520.68 |
| Proportional to square-root size | 100.00 | 28.83 | 33.49 | 502.03 |

The table's person measure assigns the family's result to all its members. It does not measure individual consumption. All shortfall means include families with no shortfall. Full estimates and survey uncertainty are in [the extension results](extensions/benefit-unit/results/outcomes.csv).

Equal adult payments perform worse than equal family payments on the family failure rate: the increase is 2.10 percentage points, with a paired standard error of 0.42 points. But they reduce the person-weighted failure rate by 2.23 points, with a standard error of 0.56 points. They also reduce the mean shortfall by $56.01, with a standard error of $4.38. There is no contradiction. Moving payments away from one-person families can create more failing families while reducing the number of people in failing families and the depth of their shortfalls.

The size-based payment reduces the person-weighted failure rate by 5.85 points relative to the equal family payment, with a standard error of 0.52 points. Its reduction in the family failure rate is much smaller: 0.67 points, with a standard error of 0.34 points. The difference between those measures matters more than a claim that one rule is best. At the common size-adjusted threshold, the family failure rate for one-person families rises from 12.42 percent under equal family payments to 20.48 percent under size-based payments. For families with three or more members, it falls from 53.47 to 41.97 percent. Equal aggregate spending redistributes protection.

A second calculation retains the original fixed $1,000 threshold for every family. Under that test, family failure rates are 22.90, 23.66 and 22.30 percent for the family, adult and size-based rules. This bridge separates a change in the payment rule from a change in the threshold. Including the two top-coded families under the capped-roster assumption leaves the substantive ordering of these measures unchanged. The all-family fixed-rule bridge also reproduces the original 22.8974 percent result and its standard error.

The extension also limits how the paper's central diagnostic should be used. The gross-pass/after-payment gap is 26.94 percent under equal family payments, 28.16 percent under equal adult payments, and 28.83 percent under size-based payments at the common size threshold. The largest gap belongs to the rule with the lowest person-weighted failure rate and the lowest mean shortfall. That rule brings everyone above the gross threshold, so its remaining failures all enter the gap. A government should use the gap to check the meaning of a coverage claim. Minimizing it alone is not a sound objective for allocating support.

These results do not settle the treatment of children, disability, care costs, local prices, or control of money within a family. They show that a post-labor guarantee needs three explicit choices: who receives a payment, how needs enter the threshold, and whose outcomes receive weight in the assessment. The debt question remains under every rule.


### 9. Implications for a post-labor transition

The first implication concerns the policy objective. An income payment, a consumption guarantee, and debt-service support are distinct commitments. A government can choose a uniform dividend as a share of common returns without promising that every family's earlier contracts remain affordable. If it instead promises a minimum standard of living, it needs evidence on prices, family needs, taxes, and essential services. If it promises to preserve debt service, it needs rules for the contracts that receive public backing.

A clear statement of that objective improves evaluation. Receipt data show whether the money arrived. Gross-resource data show whether the stated income threshold was reached. Neither alone shows whether a household retained enough for its intended consumption. At the same time, an after-debt measure should not silently convert prior borrowing choices into a complete measure of need. Reporting the measures together makes the policy choice visible.

The second implication is that income reform and debt resolution should be designed together. A long period without competitive wages changes the repayment capacity of contracts made against those wages. Temporary payment holidays may still be useful at the start, when the extent of the change is uncertain. But a permanent loss needs a rule for eventual repayment, restructuring, loss allocation, or new capital income. Merely postponing the same obligation does not establish a durable settlement.

The third implication concerns equal treatment. Supporting every inherited debt payment can protect households from abrupt losses, but it can also support high levels of prior leverage. A debt-free tenant with large rent and a borrower with a large mortgage may have similar needs while appearing very different in a debt-only test. Policy could protect a defined set of essentials, cap eligible payments, or use insolvency procedures rather than reimburse all obligations. These are design options, not reforms whose legal feasibility or effectiveness this paper has established.

The fourth implication is that the creditor side belongs in the same account. A reduction in household payments can weaken institutions that hold the loans. Public compensation can move that cost onto taxpayers or public assets. If public funds also invest in those loans, a dividend and a debt intervention may affect the same balance sheet through different routes. The present household calculation should be joined to that financial-system account before it supports a financing recommendation.

The fifth implication concerns timing. The survey combines earlier annual income with payments near the interview. A real transition programme would need current income, due dates, arrears, and renegotiation terms. Annual averages can conceal a missed payment in the first month. The data used here are suitable for a transparent distributional stress test, but they cannot establish operational payment schedules or case-level eligibility.

These points do not imply that broad cash transfers are ineffective. Every larger transfer in the experiment weakly improves the family's immediate position. Cash also permits choices that a debt-specific subsidy does not. The argument is that the meaning of success must be stated accurately and that remaining contractual obligations require a deliberate decision.

### 10. Limits and further research

The main limitation is that the economy is held fixed except for specified income entries and transfers. An economy in which AI outcompetes human labor would also change output, prices, profits, asset values, interest rates, credit supply, fiscal capacity, and family behavior. The public 2025 survey cannot identify that equilibrium. It supplies a recent distribution of inherited claims for a conditional exercise.

The experiment also retains some flows that may be linked to labor income or asset sales. The wider-flow case tests one alternative, but it does not separate every component perfectly. Before-tax income can overstate spendable resources. The zero bound removes negative remainders. The payment measure includes constructed amounts and can include mortgage escrow items. It also contains payments that borrowers would not maintain under stress. It cannot establish legal amounts due. These choices pull the comparison in different directions; the net error cannot be signed without a richer model.

Family needs are not measured. The original fixed amount per SCF family treats different sizes alike. The extension varies payments with adult count or size, but its square-root threshold remains an assumption. Neither calculation estimates a poverty threshold. Nor does it measure the quality of housing or services purchased by debt-financed assets. A policy that reduced all payments equally would have distributional consequences that this calculation does not value.

Survey standard errors address sampling and imputation under the fixed experiment. They do not capture uncertainty about AI capabilities, the timing of displacement, the persistence of nonwage income, or the policy response. A narrow standard error cannot make the future scenario probable. Small differences across scenarios should not be read as precisely estimated policy rankings.

The source comparison also has limits. Several close primary papers were read beyond their abstracts, but full text was unavailable for two relevant contributions. Their accessible abstracts are sufficient to show that the broad debt/basic-income concern and cash-support simulation are established ideas. A fuller literature review could further narrow the incremental claim. No claim of exhaustive originality or external peer review is made.

A useful extension would link household income and obligations to explicit creditor balance sheets and a dated transition path. It could compare capped payment support, restructuring, and a uniform dividend under a common fiscal budget. Such a study would need rules for default, essential consumption, asset prices, and new borrowing. It should allow its preferred policy to lose under plausible assumptions, rather than treat contractual payment continuity as an unquestioned objective.

### 11. Conclusion

A government can deliver an income payment and still leave a large difference between gross resources and resources after debt service. In the specified wage-loss experiment, that difference affects 22.9 percent of families at the central transfer and threshold. The result becomes smaller with payment relief and larger when rent or further income loss is included. A guarantee expressly defined after debt service also creates a much larger gross payment commitment than an otherwise identical gross-resource guarantee. The extension also shows why a family count, a person-weighted count, and a dollar shortfall can rank equal-budget payment rules differently. These findings do not select a benefit level. They show why a post-labor settlement must decide what it protects and how it treats the contracts inherited from the labor economy.

### Appendix A. Estimation and reproducibility

For each statistic, the point estimate is the average of its five weighted implicate estimates. For a subgroup, the numerator and denominator are calculated separately in each implicate. All published means include the specified subgroup's zero outcomes. National totals restore the factor of five in the summary weight before calculating each implicate total.

Let $Q_m$ be the statistic in implicate $m$, and let $Q_b^*$ be its value under replicate weight $b$ in the first implicate. Following the official SCF example, the estimated variance is

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(Q)=
\frac{1}{998}\sum_{b=1}^{999}(Q_b^*-\bar Q^*)^2
+\frac{6}{5}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=1}^{5}(Q_m-\bar Q)^2. \tag{3}
$$

Replicate weights equal the supplied weight times its multiplicity. Missing weight and multiplicity cells for unselected families are zero. The first-implicate family identifiers are joined explicitly; row order is not used. The coverage difference is computed as a paired household indicator, so its uncertainty includes covariance rather than adding two independent standard errors.

The release retains the selected public microdata, estimates from each implicate, all 999 replicate estimates for the principal statistics, and the complete scenario tables. The selected-data copy introduces only floating-point conversion differences of less than one millionth of a dollar in the checked source fields. The saved-replicate reconstruction can also differ in the last printed digits; agreement is tested within numerical tolerance. Full recomputation of sampling estimates requires the official replicate-weight download; reconstruction from the retained replicate estimates is available offline. These are distinct checks. The latter does not independently verify the original application of weights. The source ledger pins the download and extracted-file hashes.

The internal analysis plan was saved before study outcomes were calculated. Source and schema checks occurred first. It was not registered with an outside service. The initial full run stopped because missing replicate cells were incorrectly treated as an incomplete join. The codebook's explicit zero-weight rule resolved that error. The failure and correction remain in the decision log. No family was dropped to make the run pass.

### Appendix B. The benefit-unit extension

Let n_i denote the number of PEU members and a_i the number aged 18 or above. For each payment rule, set v_i to 1, a_i, or the square root of n_i. Given weights w_i and the retained sample, the payment coefficient is

$$
c(w)=2{,}000\frac{\sum_i w_i}{\sum_i w_i v_i},\qquad U_i(w)=c(w)v_i.
$$

The sum of weighted payments is therefore exactly 2,000 times the weighted family count under each arm. The primary threshold is F_i=1,000 sqrt(n_i). The fixed-threshold bridge sets F_i=1,000. Both use the original wage-only N_i and TPAY. The target is an allocation rule whose rate adjusts to keep this aggregate budget, rather than a benefit amount fixed in dollars outside the sample.

The coefficient is fitted anew in each of five implicates and each of 999 first-implicate replicates. Each paired contrast is also calculated within the same replicate. Equation (3) then combines sampling and imputation variance. This procedure accounts for estimation of the payment rate and covariance between arms. It does not model tax recapture, fiscal capacity, future needs or uncertainty about which scale is appropriate.

The primary sample has 4,358 families per implicate. The capped-roster check has 4,360. The smallest prespecified subgroup has 1,167 first-implicate families, above the planned 100-family interpretation threshold. Every join and roster-size check passes. The maximum relative error in matching gross payment budgets is below 3.4 times 10^-16 across all estimates. Ten tests include hand-calculated weighted allocations, the age-18 boundary, top-code exclusion, missing and zero-adult failures, and the variance formula. The all-family fixed-rule bridge reproduces the original central estimates and their standard errors without changing the original results.
